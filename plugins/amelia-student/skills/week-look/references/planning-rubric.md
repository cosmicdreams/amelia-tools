# Planning Rubric

This reference is the lookup detail behind the `week-look` skill's ranking step.
The skill body tells you *what to do, in order*; this document tells you *how to
weigh things* once you have the candidate items in hand. It holds the seven
ranking factors and the minimum-viable-week fallback.

Everything here is read-only work on material Amelia already gave you: her course
memory and her review ledger, both in her own data root. Nothing in this rubric
ever writes to a calendar or any connected account.

A note on language: spell every term out in full. No abbreviations, no acronyms,
anywhere in the answer you produce.

---

## How to use the ranking factors

You are not computing a precise numeric score and you should not show one — that
would read like a gradebook, which this is not. You are forming a ranked judgment
from seven factors, then ordering three to five items most-important first. Think
of the factors as the considerations a sharp friend would weigh out loud, not as a
spreadsheet.

The two factors that dominate almost every week are **due-date proximity** and
**grade weight**: something due soon and worth a lot belongs near the top. The
other five adjust the order, surface hidden urgency, and break ties.

---

## The seven ranking factors

### 1. Due-date proximity

How soon it is due. Sooner ranks higher. This is one of the two strongest
signals. An item due in two days almost always outranks one due in ten, all else
being equal. Use the due dates recorded in course memory; if a due date is missing
or was marked ambiguous, treat it as uncertain rather than assuming it is far off,
and say so in the plan.

### 2. Grade weight

How much of the course grade the item is worth. Heavier ranks higher. This is the
other strong signal. A twenty-percent essay outranks a two-percent quiz even when
the quiz is due a little sooner, because the cost of doing the essay badly is far
larger. Use the weights captured from the syllabus or rubric; if a weight is
unknown, note that and lean on the other factors.

### 3. Ambiguity

How unclear the requirements are — how much she still does not understand about
what the work actually asks for. More ambiguity ranks *higher*, because ambiguous
work needs to be started earlier: the first real task is figuring out what is being
asked, and that cannot be crammed. If course memory flagged the assignment as
ambiguous or under-specified, push it up the list and let the offered next action
be "pull apart what this is really asking for."

### 4. Dependency

Whether other work depends on this, or whether this depends on something else
first. An item that unblocks several others, or that must happen before she can
start the most important thing, ranks higher. A reading that a problem set assumes,
or a rubric she must understand before drafting, is a dependency: it is small on
its own but it gates a larger item, so it earns an early slot even at low weight.

### 5. Estimated effort

Roughly how long the item will take. Larger effort ranks higher *for the purpose
of starting early* — a big task needs lead time so it can be broken across several
days rather than crammed the night before. Effort does not mean "do the longest
thing first to completion"; it means a long task should appear high on the list so
she begins it in time. A short task that is also urgent can still lead the "start
first" call; use judgment.

### 6. Late penalty

How harshly lateness is punished for this item. A steep or automatic late penalty
(for example, a hard cutoff with no credit after the deadline, or a per-day
deduction) ranks the item higher as its deadline approaches, because the downside
of slipping is severe. A generous or flexible policy lowers the urgency somewhat.
Use the late policy captured in course memory; if it is unknown, treat the penalty
as significant rather than negligible.

### 7. Friction

How hard it is to *begin* — the activation cost. High friction (she dreads it, it
is vague, she does not know the first step, it requires materials she has not
gathered) is a reason to surface the item early and to make the offered next action
a tiny, concrete first move that lowers the barrier. Friction does not make
something more important on its own, but it predicts procrastination, so the plan
should defuse it by naming an easy entry point rather than letting the whole task
loom.

## Combining the factors and breaking ties

1. Start from the two dominant factors: order items by a blend of due-date
   proximity and grade weight. This gives a first-pass ranking.

2. Adjust upward for ambiguity, dependency, large estimated effort, steep late
   penalty, and high friction — each of these is a reason to start something
   sooner than its raw deadline-and-weight position would suggest.

3. Break ties with dependency first (the thing that unblocks other work wins),
   then with due-date proximity (the sooner one wins), then with grade weight.

4. Hold the final list to three to five items. If more than five qualify, keep the
   top five and drop the rest silently — do not show a long tail.

5. The **start first** pick is normally the top-ranked item. The one exception:
   when the top item is gated by an unmet dependency, the first move is the
   dependency (for example, "read chapter four first, because the problem set
   assumes it"), with a one-line note that it clears the path to the bigger task.

## The minimum viable week (the sparse-week fallback)

Some weeks are genuinely light. When almost nothing has a near deadline, do not
manufacture items to reach three. Padding a thin week with filler makes the whole
ritual feel like busywork and trains her to ignore it. Instead, give an honest
minimum:

- **Surface only what genuinely matters, even if that is a single item.** One real
  thing, named plainly, beats three invented ones.

- **Lead with the review-ledger concepts due this week.** On a quiet deadline week,
  spaced review is the highest-value use of her time, so promote it to the front of
  the answer instead of treating it as a footnote.

- **If truly nothing is due and nothing is due for review,** say so warmly and
  briefly — "Your week is genuinely light: nothing is due and nothing is up for
  review" — and offer one small, optional forward step, such as getting ahead on
  the next big assignment or capturing a syllabus she has not added yet. Offer it;
  do not perform it.

- **Never invent a deadline, a weight, or a task** to fill space. An accurate light
  week is a useful answer. A fabricated busy week is not.

The fallback's whole purpose is to keep the ritual trustworthy: when she opens a
light week and gets an honest "you are in good shape, here is the one worthwhile
thing," she learns the answer is always real — which is exactly why she keeps
asking it.
