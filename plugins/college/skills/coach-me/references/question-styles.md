# Self-Test Question Styles

This file is the lookup for the self-testing mode of the coach-me skill. It describes the five styles of test item, when to reach for each, and how to write a good one. The [tutoring modes](tutoring-modes.md#mode-two-self-testing-predict-reveal-compare) file tells you how to run the predict-reveal-compare loop around these items; this file tells you what the items themselves should look like.

The single most important instruction: **mix the styles.** A self-test made only of recall questions measures memory and nothing else, and memory is the part of understanding that fails first under exam pressure and matters least afterward. Reaching across recall, conceptual, application, and error-spotting in one batch is what reveals whether she actually understands an idea or has only memorized its surface. Write all items from her own course materials, pulled through the grounded-answer behavior — never from general knowledge that her professor did not assign.

Everything here stays inside the coaching boundary defined in the [coaching contract](../../../references/coaching-contract.md). Self-test items are about a concept she is studying; they are never the live questions on a graded take-home assessment. If she asks you to "quiz her" using the exact questions from an assignment she will submit, that is not practice — snap to the strictest tier and coach the method instead.

---

## Style one: recall

**What it tests:** whether she can retrieve a fact, definition, term, formula, date, or step from memory without prompting.

**When to use it:** as a warm-up, and for the genuine bedrock facts a field is built on — the vocabulary, the core formulas, the named theorems or events she truly must have at her fingertips. Keep recall a minority of the batch. It is necessary but it is the floor, not the goal.

**How to write a good one:** ask for the thing directly and close-ended, so right and wrong are unambiguous and her confidence rating is meaningful. "What is the definition of opportunity cost?" "State the formula for the area under a curve between two bounds." Avoid recall questions whose answer is a whole paragraph — those drift into conceptual territory and muddy the confidence signal.

---

## Style two: conceptual

**What it tests:** whether she understands *why* something is true and how ideas relate — not just that she can name it, but that she can explain it, compare it, and reason about it.

**When to use it:** for anything she will need to *use* rather than merely recite, and to catch the most common illusion of knowledge — recognizing a term while being unable to explain it.

**How to write a good one:** ask her to explain, compare, contrast, or justify. "Why does increasing the sample size narrow the confidence interval?" "What is the difference between correlation and causation, and why does it matter for this study?" "Explain in your own words why this reaction is exothermic." The phrase "in your own words" is doing real work here — it forces production, which is the only honest test of conceptual grasp.

---

## Style three: application

**What it tests:** whether she can take a concept she understands in the abstract and *use* it on a new, concrete case she has not seen before.

**When to use it:** for any course where the exam will present unfamiliar problems — which is most of them. Understanding a method and being able to deploy it on a fresh instance are different skills, and application items are the only ones that test the second.

**How to write a good one:** give her a new scenario and ask her to apply the concept to it. Use *different numbers and a different instance* than any worked example she has seen — this doubles as boundary safety, because an application item built on a fresh case is by construction not the assigned problem. "A firm faces this new cost structure — which option should it choose, and why?" "Given this new data set, which test would you run, and what would the result tell you?" The case must be new; re-asking a worked example tests memory of that example, not application.

---

## Style four: error-spotting

**What it tests:** whether her understanding is robust enough to recognize when something is *wrong* — to catch a flawed step, a false claim, or a misapplied rule.

**When to use it:** for concepts where the typical mistakes are predictable, and to probe depth. Spotting an error is harder than producing a correct answer, because it requires holding the correct model firmly enough to notice a deviation from it. It is also excellent calibration bait: a confident miss on an error-spotting item is a precise map of a misconception.

**How to write a good one:** present a worked solution, an argument, or a claim that contains exactly one (or a known number of) deliberate flaws, and ask her to find and fix them. "Here is a student's proof — find the step where it goes wrong and explain why." "This argument concludes that the policy will lower prices. What is wrong with the reasoning?" Make the flaw a *plausible* one — the actual mistake students make — not an obvious typo. Build these from a different, analogous case, never from her own graded work, so you are not quietly correcting her submission under the label of a quiz.

---

## Style five: exam simulation

**What it tests:** whether she can perform under the actual conditions of her exam — the time limit, the question formats, the closed-book constraint, the pressure.

**When to use it:** late in her preparation, once the earlier styles show she has the material, and only when she is studying for an exam (a setting where, by the contract's medium tier, full answers and worked solutions are legitimate because she will soon be on her own). This is where you rehearse the test itself.

**How to write a good one:** mirror the real exam's structure as closely as her materials reveal it — the question types her syllabus or past papers show, a realistic count, and a clock. Tell her the time budget up front and hold her to it. Then debrief like the rest of the self-test: confidence before reveal, name the calibration gaps, and close the loop on what she missed. The value is partly the content and partly the dress rehearsal — finding out now that she runs out of time on the long-answer section is worth more than another quiet study hour.

A boundary note specific to this style: an exam simulation is built from *representative* questions in the style of her exam, drawn from practice material or your own analogous construction. It is never a copy of a live take-home exam she will submit. If the "simulation" she hands you is the real assessment, it is graded work — coach it, do not answer it.

---

## Choosing the mix for a batch

A solid default batch of six items: one recall warm-up, two conceptual, two application, one error-spotting — then close the loop on whatever she missed and re-test those with *fresh* items in a different style than the one she failed, so she learns the idea and not the question. Save full exam simulation for a dedicated late-stage session rather than folding it into a mixed batch. Always weight the mix toward the styles that test understanding over the one that tests memory.
