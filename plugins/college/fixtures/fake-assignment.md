# Research Paper — Environmental Science 210

**Instructor:** Professor Maren Holloway
**Weight in final grade:** twenty-five percent total (proposal five percent, final paper twenty percent)
**Final paper length:** eight to ten pages, double spaced, not counting the title page or the list of sources

## Due dates

- **Proposal (one page):** due Thursday of the fifth week of the term, at the start of class (eleven o'clock in the morning).
- **Final paper:** due Tuesday of the twelfth week of the term, by eleven o'clock at night, uploaded to the course page as a portable document format file.

Remember the late-work policy from the course outline: written work loses ten percent of its possible points for each calendar day it is late, and it is not accepted after three calendar days late. If you need an extension, contact Professor Holloway before the deadline.

## The assignment

Choose one specific, local environmental question and write an evidence-based argument about it. "Local" means it affects a particular place you can name — a watershed, a city, a coastline, a forest, a farming region — not the entire planet at once. Your paper should make a clear claim and support it with evidence drawn from the scientific concepts we have studied.

Your paper must do all of the following:

1. **State a clear, arguable claim.** Not "climate change is real" (too broad and not arguable) but something specific you could be wrong about, such as "the city of Riverbend should replace its combined sewer system because heavier storms are overwhelming it."
2. **Ground the argument in course concepts.** Connect your local question to at least two of the major ideas from the course — for example the water cycle, energy flow, biodiversity, the carbon cycle, or pollution pathways. Name the concepts explicitly and explain how they apply.
3. **Use and interpret real evidence.** Include at least one data figure (a graph, a table, or a map) that you read and explain in your own words. You may use a figure from a published source, but you must say what it shows and why it supports your claim — do not just paste it in.
4. **Consider a counterargument.** Name the strongest objection to your claim and respond to it honestly. A paper that pretends there is no other side scores lower.
5. **Cite your sources.** Use at least four credible sources, at least two of which must be peer-reviewed scientific articles. Cite them consistently in any standard style, and include a list of sources at the end.

## What to hand in for the proposal

The one-page proposal, due in the fifth week, should contain:

- Your specific local question and your working claim (two or three sentences).
- The place it concerns and why it matters there.
- Which two or more course concepts you expect to use.
- Two or three sources you have already found, with a sentence on what each contributes.

The proposal is graded on whether the question is specific and workable and whether you have started finding real evidence — not on whether your claim is final. You may change your claim later.

## Formatting requirements

- Eight to ten pages of body text, double spaced, twelve-point standard serif font, one-inch margins.
- A title page with your name, the course, the date, and your paper's title.
- Page numbers on every page after the title page.
- A list of sources at the end, alphabetized, in a single consistent style.
- Submit as a single portable document format file uploaded to the course page.

## Grading rubric (one hundred points total)

The final paper is scored out of one hundred points across five categories. Read this rubric before you start and again before you submit.

### Claim and argument — twenty-five points

- **Excellent (twenty-two to twenty-five points):** The claim is specific, arguable, and local. The argument is logical from start to finish, and every section serves the claim.
- **Proficient (eighteen to twenty-one points):** The claim is clear and mostly specific. The argument is sound with one or two gaps.
- **Developing (twelve to seventeen points):** The claim is present but broad or only partly arguable. The argument wanders or has weak links.
- **Beginning (zero to eleven points):** No clear claim, or the paper describes a topic instead of arguing a position.

### Use of course concepts — twenty points

- **Excellent (eighteen to twenty points):** Two or more course concepts are named explicitly and applied accurately and meaningfully to the local question.
- **Proficient (fourteen to seventeen points):** At least two concepts are used, but one is applied loosely or named without much explanation.
- **Developing (nine to thirteen points):** Only one concept is genuinely used, or concepts are mentioned but not connected to the argument.
- **Beginning (zero to eight points):** Course concepts are absent or used incorrectly.

### Evidence and data interpretation — twenty points

- **Excellent (eighteen to twenty points):** At least one data figure is included, read correctly, and explained in the student's own words in direct support of the claim.
- **Proficient (fourteen to seventeen points):** A data figure is included and explained, with minor errors or a loose link to the claim.
- **Developing (nine to thirteen points):** A figure is included but barely explained, or evidence is described without a figure.
- **Beginning (zero to eight points):** No real evidence, or evidence is pasted in without interpretation.

### Counterargument — fifteen points

- **Excellent (thirteen to fifteen points):** The strongest objection is named fairly and answered with reasoning or evidence.
- **Proficient (ten to twelve points):** A counterargument is addressed, but it is a weak one or the response is thin.
- **Developing (six to nine points):** A counterargument is mentioned but not really answered.
- **Beginning (zero to five points):** No counterargument is considered.

### Writing, sources, and formatting — twenty points

- **Excellent (eighteen to twenty points):** Clear, well-organized prose; at least four credible sources including two peer-reviewed articles, cited consistently; all formatting requirements met.
- **Proficient (fourteen to seventeen points):** Generally clear writing; sources present and mostly meeting the requirement; minor formatting or citation slips.
- **Developing (nine to thirteen points):** Writing is unclear in places; sources are too few or weak; several formatting problems.
- **Beginning (zero to eight points):** Hard to follow; missing the source requirement; formatting requirements largely ignored.

## How your final score becomes a grade

Add the points from all five categories for a score out of one hundred, then apply the letter grade scale from the course outline. For example, a paper that earns ninety-one points out of one hundred would be an A minus.
